Stocks

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

Both the Northern and Southern Stocks of Monkfish received a Low sensitivity score. Certainties were 99% and 98% respectively (Figure 33.1). For both stocks, the only attribute that received a score greater than 2.5 was Population Growth Rate (2.6 for both stocks) (Figure 33.1). Monkfish mature slowly, reaching maturity at 4-5 years (Richards, Nitschke, and Sosebee 2008). Maximum ages range from 7 years for males to 14 years for females (Northeast Fisheries Science Center 2010).

Monkfish also received a Low sensitivity score in the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment for the Northeast Shelf. Like in this assessment, this result was driven by Population Growth Rate (Hare et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Mean Exposure for Monkfish was Moderate throughout its range (2.02 +/- 0.95). Scores were also Moderate when averaged across both the Northern and Southern stocks (1.99 +/- 0.96 and 2.08 +/- 0.95, respectively) (Figure 33.1). Exposure was greatest (Very High) over Georges Bank and Moderate in Southern New England and along the coast of the Gulf of Maine (Figure 33.5). This was driven by bottom temperatures, oxygen, and salinity, as well as zooplankton biomass exposure. Exposure was also moderate on the shelf break due to high exposure to changes in bottom aragonite solubility (Figure 33.4).

In the previous Climate Vulnerability Assessment for the Northeast Shelf, monkfish received a High Exposure score. This previous score was driven by sea surface temperature, ocean acidification, and currents (Hare et al. 2016).

Mean Exposure for Monkfish was Moderate throughout its range (2.02 +/- 0.95). Scores were also Moderate when averaged across both the Northern and Southern stocks (1.99 +/- 0.96 and 2.08 +/- 0.95, respectively) (Figure 33.1). Exposure was greatest (Very High) over Georges Bank and Moderate in Southern New England and along the coast of the Gulf of Maine (Figure 33.5). This was driven by bottom temperatures, oxygen, and salinity, as well as zooplankton biomass exposure. In the previous Climate Vulnerability Assessment for the Northeast Shelf, monkfish received a High Exposure score. This previous score was driven by sea surface temperature, ocean acidification, and currents (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

88% of data quality scores were greater than or equal to 2, indicating strong data quality. No data gaps (mean attribute data quality score < 2) were identified (Figure 33.1).

Model Confidence Score:

The monkfish ensemble model received a 2.4 (+/- 0.4) model confidence score, indicating moderate confidence in the model. The final model met experts’ expectations, representing the known distribution and drivers well. The ensemble model AUC score was 0.94, indicating that the model had excellent predictive performance.

Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

Monkfish lost significant amounts of habitat between 1993 - 2023, particularly off of southern New England and at the southern edge of their range. Monkfish have gained habitat in the Gulf of Maine and total habitat area has increased in recent years (Figure 33.7). The weighted centroid has shifted 51 km northeast between 1993 and 2023.

Under near future ocean conditions, the recent habitat growth trend is expected to continue. Monkfish habitat is predicted to expand south of Georges Bank and at the southern edge of their range (Figure 33.7). The weighted centroid is expected to shift 17 km southwest, back towards its 1993 location.

In the previous Assessment, monkfish received a High Distributional Vulnerability rank because of their habitat preferences (generalists) and seasonal migrations (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Climate change is predicted to negatively impact both the northern and southern stocks of Monkfish. Certainties were 94% and 87% respectively. Monkfish are most abundant on Georges Bank when bottom temperatures are between 5-14 C, suggesting that warming temperatures would decrease the amount of preferred habitat (Siemann et al. 2018). Experts in the previous Assessment predicted that climate change would have a neutral impact on Monkfish with a high degree of uncertainty (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

In Icelandic waters, monkfish distribution changes and increased stock size have been correlated with rising water temperatures and expanding suitable habitat (Solmundsson, Jonsson, and Bjornsson 2007). Monkfish movement and relative abundance on Georges Bank is driven by seasonal changes in bottom temperature. Their distribution may shift in response to future changes in thermal habitat, highlighting the need for further management strategies (Siemann et al. 2018). Maximum size of monkfish in the Northwest Atlantic has decreased, and is related to seasonality and bottom temperature (Charbonneau et al. 2020).

Life History Synopsis

Monkfish are a bottom dwelling fish ranging from the northern Gulf of St. Lawrence to Cape Hatteras. The commercial fishery operates from Maine to North Carolina (Steimle, Morse, and Johnson 1999). Spawning takes place from spring through early autumn, progressing from south to north over a 6 month period (Richards, Nitschke, and Sosebee 2008). Pelagic larvae hatch from egg veils (up 12 m long and 1.5 m wide) 1-3 weeks after being laid, depending on temperature. Larvae remain in the egg veil 2-3 days after hatching. Monkfish larvae feed on zooplankton, including copepods, crustacean larvae, and chaetognaths (Bigelow and Schroeder 1953). Larval to juvenile transition occurs at 5-10 cm TL (total length) when the elongate fins and body gradually assume the adult form (Northeast Fisheries Science Center 2010). Once larvae transition to juveniles, they move to the benthos. Juveniles and adults settle on mud, gravelly sand, algae, and rock substrates (Steimle, Morse, and Johnson 1999). Once moved to the bottom, juveniles can eat sand lance, but invertebrates such as red shrimp and squid make up the majority of their diet. The amount of invertebrates eaten by juveniles decreases as they get older (Steimle, Morse, and Johnson 1999). Monkfish are ambush predators and opportunistic feeders; prey found in their stomachs include a variety of benthic and pelagic species. Monkfish are also cannibalistic and can eat sea birds and diving ducks. Adults spend most of their time resting on the bottom, but have been reported at the surface after a storm event, preying on seabirds (Richards, Nitschke, and Sosebee 2008). Large monkfish have few predators, but smaller monkfish are common prey for swordfish, sharks, and skates (Steimle, Morse, and Johnson 1999). Monkfish move by slow swimming or by using their sturdy pectoral fins to “walk.” Despite slow speeds, juveniles and adults migrate in response to thermal conditions, spawning, or food availability (Steimle, Morse, and Johnson 1999). Monkfish were found on Georges Bank in summer and early fall, and found in deeper waters/on the edge of the bank in winter months, suggesting that movements may be influenced by temperature (Rountree, Gr"oger, and Martins 2006). Age at maturity is 4-5 years, longer for females and for the Mid-Atlantic stock (Richards, Nitschke, and Sosebee 2008). Maximum age is listed at 7 years for males and 12-14 years for females (Northeast Fisheries Science Center 2010). Monkfish in the Northwest Atlantic are divided into two stocks: the Gulf of Maine/Northern Georges Bank and Southern Georges Bank/Mid-Atlantic. Both stocks do not have stock assessments that provide indices on overfishing and overfished status, although an index based assessment is used for catch advice (Deroba 2022).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

Within the Northern and Southern stocks of Monkfish, monthly exposures ranged from Low to High (Figure 33.10). In the Northern Stock, Exposure was Moderate from May to November and increased to High in August. In the Southern Stock, Exposure was High from August through October. These patterns were driven by increases in bottom temperature and oxygen exposure as the year progressed, and sharp increases in medium zooplankton biomass in late summer (Figure 33.9).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Several factors were considered important dynamic drivers of Monkfish distributions: bottom temperature, oxygen, salinity, and large zooplankton biomass (Figure 33.11). Adult monkfish are demersal (Richards et al. 2008) and movement patterns have been associated with bottom temperatures (Siemann et al. 2018). Monkfish are generalist predators but larvae prey on zooplankton (Bigelow and Schroeder 1953). When only these factors were used to calculate Exposure, scores remained Moderate both throughout the species range and within the stocks (Figure 33.12, Figure 33.13).
