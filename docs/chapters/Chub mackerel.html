Stocks

Chub mackerel is managed as a single stock or stock information was not considered for this iteration of the CVA.

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

Chub mackerel were given a Low sensitivity score, with 99% certainty according to bootstrap analysis (Figure 27.1). The only attribute that received a score greater than 2.5 was Stock Size. This is likely because no stock assessment for chub mackerel exists (Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council (MAFMC) 2025).

Chub mackerel were not assessed in the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment, nor any other recent Assessment (Hare et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Mean Exposure for Chub mackerel was High (2.89 +/- 0.69) (Figure 27.1). Exposure was High throughout most of the species range, but was Very High south of Long Island and Cape Cod, and Low off the coast of Chesapeake Bay (Figure 27.4). This was driven by surface temperature, salinity, and pH exposures, plus high exposures in phytoplankton productivity variables (all size classes) around Hudson Canyon and off the coasts of Delaware and Virginia (Figure 27.3).

Chub mackerel were not assessed in the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment, nor any other recent Assessment (Hare et al. 2016).

Vulnerability:

Mean Vulnerability for Chub mackerel was Low (2.89 +/- 0.69) due to Low sensitivity scores (Figure 27.1). Vulnerability was Low throughout the species’ range, except south of Long Island and Cape Cod where Vulnerability was Moderate (Figure 27.5).

Chub mackerel were not assessed in the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment, nor any other recent Assessment (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

Only 68% of data quality scores were greater than or equal to 2, indicating poor data quality. Data gaps (mean attribute data quality score < 2) included Complexity in Reproductive Strategy, Dispersal of Early Life Stages, Egg and Larval Survival and Settlement Requirements, Other Stressors, and Stock Size/Status (Figure 27.1).

Model Confidence Score:

The chub mackerel ensemble model received a mean model confidence score of 2.1 (+/- 0.6), indicating moderate confidence in the final model. Experts thought that the model generally described chub mackerel distributions well, but the low sample size drove scores down. The ensemble AUC score for chub mackerel was 0.89 indicating that the final model had good predictive performance.

Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

Chub mackerel experienced significant habitat expansion between 1993 and 2023. Core habitats expanded further north along the coast of New Jersey and their range expanded further east along the coast of southern New England (Figure 27.6). The weighted centroid shifted 27 km north-northeast between 1993 and 2023.

Under near future climate conditions, chub mackerel habitat is expected to remain similar. Chub mackerel are predicted to lose habitat on the continental shelf break, but gain habitat nearshore and on the shelf itself as the northern and eastern edges of their distribution continue to shift further north and east respectively (Figure 27.6). The weighted centroid is predicted to shift 34 km westward, reflecting the increase in inshore habitat use.

Chub mackerel were not assessed previously so no previous ranks are available.

Directional Effect:

Experts predicted that climate change would have a neutral effect on chub mackerel. Expert certainty was 87%. Variations in abundance have been observed and are likely due to oceanographic processes due to their pelagic lifestyle, but the exact drivers are unclear (Hern’andez and Ortega 2000).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

Fluctuations in Atlantic chub mackerel abundance have been reported in the Mid-Atlantic and New England, and may result from environmental influences such as temperature and upwelling. Oceanographic processes likely influence recruitment and distribution, as all life stages of chub mackerel are fully pelagic (Hern’andez and Ortega 2000). In the eastern Atlantic, chub mackerel distributions have shifted northward, with a delayed and prolonged spawning season with increasing latitude (Dom’inguez-Petit et al. 2022). In the Alboran Sea, chub mackerel abundance is vulnerable to variations in sea surface temperature, salinity, currents, and wind patterns (Ayman et al. 2024).

Life History Synopsis

Atlantic chub mackerel is a schooling pelagic species ranging from Nova Scotia to Argentina, including the Gulf of America, and are found on the continental shelf at depths of 250-300m (Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council (MAFMC) 2025). They are batch spawners, spawning January through April from North Carolina through the Gulf of America (Daley 2018). Spawning generally occurs in waters greater than 10°C and is possibly triggered by water temperatures between 15- 20°C (Hern’andez and Ortega 2000). Chub mackerel eggs develop for approximately 53-56 hrs at 18-20°C. Larval stages last between 16-24 days, developing faster at higher temperatures (Hern’andez and Ortega 2000). Atlantic chub mackerel grow rapidly during the first year of life, reaching maturity around 2 years of age (Daley 2018) and can live up to 13 years (Hern’andez and Ortega 2000). Chub mackerel are generalist predators of zooplankton and other pelagic prey including mysids, euphausids, decapod larvae, copepods, amphipods, small fish, cephalopods. Cannibalism has been observed in this species (Hern’andez and Ortega 2000). There is little data on chub mackerel predation in the Northwest Atlantic. The Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council funded a study on diet composition of marlins and tunas and found very few chub mackerel in the stomachs of yellowfin tuna and white marlin (Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council (MAFMC) 2025). In 2019, the Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council approved Amendment 21 to the Mackerel, Squid, and Butterfish Fishery Management Plan. This Amendment established conservation and management measures for Atlantic chub mackerel in the Northwest Atlantic (Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council (MAFMC) 2019). There is currently no stock assessment of chub mackerel in the Mid-Atlantic. Biomass is assumed to be at a sustainable level based on stock productivity and low fishery capacity (Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council (MAFMC) 2025).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

Monthly exposure ranged from Moderate to Very High (Figure 27.9). Scores were Very High in May due to coincident spikes in phytoplankton biomass exposure across size classes. Exposure declined to Moderate in July and August due to declines in surface salinity exposure (Figure 27.8).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Sea surface temperature, surface salinity, and diazotroph primary productivity were important dynamic drivers of chub mackerel distributions on the Northeast Shelf (Figure 27.10). Chub mackerel are generalist zooplankton predators and oceanographic processes play a role in spawning and their distribution (Hern’andez and Ortega 2000). When only these factors are included in the exposure calculation, mean exposure drops from High to Moderate (1.54 +/- 0.83) (Figure 27.12). Exposure in this scenario is High off the coasts of Long Island and New Jersey, where high surface temperature and salinity exposures overlap, but Low elsewhere (Figure 27.11).
