Stocks

Atlantic herring is managed as a single stock or stock information was not considered for this iteration of the CVA.

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

Atlantic herring had a Moderate sensitivity score, with a certainty of 90%. This score was driven by two attributes: Stock Size (4) and Spawning Cycle (2.8) (Figure 15.1).

Spawning success and recruitment is highly correlated with temperatures. The optimal spawning temperature window has shortened over the past 30 years (Kanwit and Libby 2009; Molina 2025). Mismatches between larvae and prey abundance lead to higher mortality (Randall et al. 2022). As of the 2024 assessment, herring are overfished but not experiencing overfishing (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2024).

In the previous assessment, herring received a Low sensitivity score. This score was driven by Early Life History Requirements, citing the need for demersal spawning habitat (Hare et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Mean total exposure for Atlantic herring was High (2.97 +/- 0.32) (Figure 15.1). Exposure was uniform across the continental shelf, with Very High exposure on Georges Bank, and Low to Moderate Exposure south of Cape Cod and east of the Chesapeake Bay (Figure 15.4). These patterns were driven by very high exposure due to sea surface temperature, salinity, and pH (Figure 15.3).

In the previous Assessment, Atlantic Herring also received a High exposure score, driven by surface temperatures and ocean acidification (Hare et al. 2016).

Vulnerability:

Mean Vulnerability for Atlantic Herring was Moderate (5.95 +/- 0.64) (Figure 15.1). Vulnerability was uniform across the shelf when all factors were considered, with peaks of High Vulnerability off Georges Bank (Figure 15.5).

In the previous Assessment, Herring received a Low Vulnerability Score. The change in this assessment is due to an increase in the Sensitivity Score (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

95% of data quality scores were greater than or equal to 2, indicating excellent data quality. No data gaps (mean attribute data quality score < 2) were identified for this species (Figure 15.1).

Model Confidence Score:

The herring ensemble model received a model confidence score of 1.6 (+/- 0.3), indicating low confidence in the model. While the model results represented general distribution patterns, experts noted that residuals (differences between observed and predicted values) were high in March and September. This was likely due to herring catchability in the surveys and interannual variability in migration timing. The ensemble model’s AUC was 0.73, indicating fair performance.

Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

The weighted distribution centroid for Atlantic herring has shifted northward by 30 km since 1993. This is due to the northward shift of the southern edges of herring core habitat and their entire range. As a result, herring habitat area has declined significantly between 1993 and 2023 (Figure 15.6).

However, herring habitats are predicted to expand slightly in the next decade. Habitat areas are expected to significantly increase between 2025 and 2035. Core habitat in the Gulf of Maine is predicted to expand southward, resulting in a nearly 4 km southward shift in the predicted distribution weighted centroid (Figure 15.6).

Herring received a High Distributional Vulnerability Rank in the previous assessment due to high mobility and larval duration (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Herring are very likely to be negatively impacted by future climate change along the Northeast Shelf (100% certainty in expert scores). Warming temperatures and increasing bottom salinities are decreasing the amount of favorable habitat for herring (Suca et al. 2021). Mismatches between larvae and prey abundances have resulted in slower growth and higher larval mortality (Randall et al. 2022). The 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment for the Northeast Shelf also suggested that herring would be negatively impacted, but with more uncertainty (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

A number of Atlantic herring life history parameters are linked to drivers of climate change. Climate-related mismatches between Atlantic herring larval feeding and prey abundance have resulted in slower growth and higher mortality (Randall et al. 2022). J. P. Gröger, Kruse, and Rohlf (2010) found that lags in the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) and Atlantic Multi-decadal Oscillation (AMO) were the primary drivers of Atlantic herring recruitment. Winter NAO has also indirectly influenced recruitment in multiple herring stocks (Axenrot and Hansson 2003; Oskarsson and Taggart 2010; Lynam et al. 2005) and may be used to predict recruitment with lead times up to 5 years (J. P. Gröger, Kruse, and Rohlf 2010). Atlantic herring recruitment is also influenced by larval temperatures, the Gulf Stream Index, and food availability from water mass movement (Molina 2025). Favorable habitat for Atlantic herring in the Northwest Atlantic is currently decreasing with rising temperatures and bottom salinity (Suca et al. 2021), with herring populations in warmer waters responding more negatively to increasing temperatures than those in colder waters (Beaudry-Sylvestre, Benoît, and Hutchings 2024). Herring larvae in the Baltic Sea are facing more heat stress and cardiac dysfunction with an increase in the number of marine heat waves (M. Gröger et al. 2024). Migration and distribution of Atlantic herring is also linked with thermal oceanic fronts between colder, less saline continental shelf water and warmer, more salient slope water (where plankton are also usually more abundant) (Kelly and Moring 1986).

Life History Synopsis

Atlantic herring in the Northwest Atlantic reside from southern Labrador in Canada to Cape Hatteras, NC (Stevenson and Scott 2005). They spawn primarily on Jeffreys Ledge and Georges Bank between July and November, with peaks in August - September in the Gulf of Maine. However, recent research has suggested that the optimal spawning temperature window has shortened over the past 30 years (Kanwit and Libby 2009; Molina 2025). Early life history development is inversely related to water temperature, with slower growth occurring at higher temperatures (Stevenson and Scott 2005). Eggs hatch after 6-15 days, and larval development time ranges from 4-8 months. Larvae absorb their yolk sac within 2.5 - 6 days, at which point the larvae become pelagic and can be transported away from or retained near spawning habitats, depending on prevailing current systems (Lough and Cohen 1982). Predation of eggs is linked to high mortality. Predators include haddock, cod, mackerel, winter flounder, moon snails, hermit crabs and starfish. Strong haddock recruitment has been linked to low abundances of larval herring (Boyar et al. 1973; Burbank et al. 2023). Larval herring prey on copepods, with size depending on the size of the larvae (Sherman and Honey 1971). Juveniles form large schools in coastal waters (i.e. estuaries and embayments) throughout the Gulf of Maine in the spring and summer, and overwinter in deep bays or offshore (Stevenson and Scott 2005). Juveniles and adults are planktivorous and opportunistic feeders. As they grow and the size of their jaws increases, they consume larger organisms. Their diet therefore varies with season, their age and size, and location. (Stevenson and Scott 2005; Sherman and Perkins 1971). A lack of availability of preferred prey in adults (due to strong mixing in the water column) can lead to suboptimal feeding and slow growth resulting in increased mortality from starvation and predation (Stevenson and Scott 2005). Adult herring make extensive seasonal migrations between summer spawning grounds on Georges Bank and in the Gulf of Maine and overwintering areas in southern New England and the Mid-Atlantic region (Stevenson and Scott 2005). There are two major spawning stocks in the Northwest Atlantic: the Gulf of Maine and Georges Bank/Nantucket Shoals. Stocks are separated based on larval distribution patterns, spawning times/locations, and growth rates (Iles and Sinclair 1982; Boyar et al. 1973; Anthony and Waring 1980). Both stocks are currently assessed in the U.S. as a single coastal stock complex managed by the New England Fishery Management Council. As of the most recent assessment (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2024), the stock is overfished but not experiencing overfishing based on the biological reference points for spawning stock biomass (SSB) and fishing mortality (F).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

Monthly exposure was consistently High. There was no variation across months (Figure 15.9; Figure 15.8).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Sea surface temperature, surface salinity, and mixed layer depth were all important drivers of herring distributions (Figure 15.10). Temperature and salinity have been linked to favorable habitats for herring (Suca et al. 2021) and increased mixing has been linked to decreased prey availability and increased mortality (Stevenson and Scott 2005). When these factors alone were used to calculate exposure, mean Exposure dropped to Moderate (1.72 +/- 0.83) (Figure 15.11). Exposure due to these variables was greatest in the Gulf of Maine and in the middle of the continental shelf (Figure 15.12).
