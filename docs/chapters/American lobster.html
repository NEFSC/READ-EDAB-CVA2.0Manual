Stocks

American Lobster is managed as a single stock or stock information was not considered for this iteration of the CVA.

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

The Gulf of Maine/Georges Bank stock of American lobster received a High sensitivity score and the Southern New England stock scored Very High. Certainty was high for both stocks, with 99% certainty for the Gulf of Maine/Georges Bank stock and 92% certainty for the Southern New England stock (Figure 11.1).

Population growth rate and other stressors drove the scores for both stocks. Relationships to calcified organisms contributed to the High score for the Gulf of Maine/Georges Bank stock, and received a score of 3.5 for the Southern New England stock. Stock size had the highest score for the Southern New England Stock (Figure 11.1).

Lobsters are long-lived, slow growing species that can reach ages of 50+ years. Disease and the introduction of new predators such as black sea bass and spiny dogfish impact lobsters across their range (Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission (ASMFC) 2025; Goldstein et al. 2025). American lobsters are omnivores but diets are dominated by mollusks (Elner and Campbell 1987), which are likely to be impacted by ocean acidification.

Sensitivity has increased since the last assessment, where lobsters received a Moderate score. Population growth rate and other stressors drove scores in both assessments (Hare et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Mean exposure for American lobster across its range was Moderate (2.11 +/- 0.95) (Figure 11.1). Exposure was Very High over Georges Bank and High in Southern New England and along the coast of the Gulf of Maine. Exposure was also High along the continental shelf break (Figure 11.4). These patterns were driven by bottom temperature, oxygen, salinity, and aragonite saturation exposure (Figure 11.3).

In the previous Assessment, American lobster received a High exposure score driven by sea surface temperature and ocean acidification (Hare et al. 2016).

Vulnerability:

Mean vulnerability for the American lobster was Moderate (6.33 +/- 2.85) across its range (Figure 11.1). Vulnerability was Very High over Georges Bank and High along the coast of the Gulf of Maine, in Southern New England, and along the continental shelf break (Figure 11.5).

In the previous Assessment, American lobster also received a Moderate Vulnerability score (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

The percent of data quality scores greater than 2 was 97% for the Gulf of Maine/Georges Bank stock and 95% for the Southern New England stock, indicating excellent data quality. For both stocks, Stock Size/Status was the only data gap (mean attribute data quality score < 2) (Figure 11.1).

Model Confidence Score:

The mean model confidence score for American Lobster was 1.45 (+/- 0.45), indicating that experts had low confidence in the model. Experts stated that the model missed seasonal inshore and offshore migrations. The ensemble model’s AUC was 0.86, indicating good predictive performance.

Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

Between 1993 and 2023, the weighted centroid shifted nearly 29 km northeast. There were significant declines in lobster habitat along the southern and western edges of their distribution (Figure 11.6).

The American lobster weighted centroid is predicted to shift 4.5 km west-southwest under near future ocean conditions. Distributions are expected to expand westward and south (Figure 11.6).

Directional Effect:

Both stocks are likely to be negatively impacted by climate change (certainties range from 81 – 100% based on bootstrap analysis of expert scores). Increased thermal stress and disease will continue to decrease the productivity of the stocks. In addition, new demersal predators such as black sea bass and spiny dogfish will increase predation pressures of juvenile and adult lobster. The directional effect was estimated to be neutral in the previous Assessment (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

The Northwest Atlantic has been warming at an exceptionally fast rate (Saba et al. 2016), which has contributed to declines in fecundity and population size in Southern New England (Goldstein, Zarrella-Smith, and Pugh 2022) while the fishery in the central Gulf of Maine has increased dramatically (Le Bris et al. 2018). Temperatures have increased in the lobster’s inshore range, and evidence suggests that the productivity of the stock is severely compromised with increased thermal stress and disease (Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission (ASMFC) 2025). The assemblage of predators of American lobster has shifted with warming conditions in the Gulf of Maine, with benthic and demersal predators, such as black sea bass (Centropristis striata) and spiny dogfish (Squalus acanthias) becoming increasingly present throughout the lobster’s range, taking the place of traditional predators, such as Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua). The effects of invasive and range-expanding species may also lead to greater predation risk for juvenile lobsters (Goldstein et al. 2025).

Life History Synopsis

American lobsters inhabit the coastal and oceanic waters of the Atlantic Ocean from Labrador to North Carolina. They are widely distributed within most of the coastal waters in the Northeast U.S. with some major offshore concentrations along the outer edge of the Continental Shelf and upper slope between Georges Bank and Delaware Bay (Mackenzie and Moring 1985). Spawning takes place during a 4 month period from late May through much of September, when females leave their solitary shelters about 7 days before molting to share the shelter of a dominant male (Factor 1995) (Mackenzie and Moring 1985). Lobster larvae are planktonic for 6-8 weeks (Factor 1995) and travel from surface waters offshore to inshore areas of Southern New England and the Gulf of Maine. Larval distribution is affected by the distribution of spawning females and by surface current velocity and direction, temperature, salinity, light intensity, and hydrostatic pressure (Katz, Cobb, and Spaulding 1994) (Harding and Trites 1988) (Hudson and Fradette 1993; Mackenzie and Moring 1985). Larval survival is likely affected by pollution of the marine environment by human activity, especially coastal waters (Connor 1972). There is an inverse relationship between temperature and the time from hatching to the postlarval stage (when they settle). Larvae take as few as 11 days at 22°C and as many as 54 days at 10°C to reach the postlarval stage. It is speculated that thermoclines are a negative cue for larvae/post larvae settlement (Factor 1995; Templeman 1936). Juvenile lobsters utilize shelters (rocks and vegetation) in the benthic habitat, and may also burrow in mud (Wahle and Steneck 1991). Juveniles are omnivorous but also scavenge for food. Much of their diet consists of bottom invertebrates, crabs, urchins, mussels, polychaetes, periwinkles, and sea stars, but they may also consume fish and plants (Lawton 1987). Adult lobsters are also considered omnivores and scavengers . However, their diet is dominated by mollusks (especially mussels), echinoderms, and polychaetes (Elner and Campbell 1987). Juveniles and adults prefer bottom water temperatures of 8-14°C (Mackenzie and Moring 1985). Inshore, adult lobsters appear to have a limited home range of about 2.2 km, however offshore lobsters tend to migrate greater distances (up to 345 km in 71 days) (Mackenzie and Moring 1985). American lobster are managed by the Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission as three stock units based on life history differences: Gulf of Maine, Georges Bank, and southern New England (Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission (ASMFC) 2025).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

Monthly exposure scores ranged from Moderate to Low (Figure 11.9). Scores were Low from February to April, corresponding to a decline in bottom temperature and oxygen exposure (Figure 11.9, Figure 11.8). Scores were Moderate for the remainder of the year (Figure 11.9).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Bottom temperature, oxygen, and salinity, in addition to net primary production and downward carbon flux were all important dynamic drivers of lobster distributions (Figure 11.10). Lobsters are omnivorous scavengers (Elner and Campbell 1987). They prefer colder waters and utilize rocky and vegetative habitats for cover (Wahle and Steneck 1991). When only these factors were used to calculate exposure, scores remained Moderate (1.99 +/- 0.96) (Figure 11.12, Figure 11.11).
