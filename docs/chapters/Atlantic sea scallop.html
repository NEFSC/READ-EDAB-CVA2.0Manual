Stocks

Atlantic sea scallop is managed as a single stock or stock information was not considered for this iteration of the CVA.

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

Both the Georges Bank and Mid Atlantic Bight stock units of Atlantic Sea Scallop received a High sensitivity score (Figure 18.1). Certainties for both stocks were 100%. Relationship to Calcified Organisms (4) and Adult Mobility (3.8) drove the scores in both stocks. Scores for each attribute were the same for both stocks (Figure 18.1).

Scallops are subject to stress due to increasing ocean acidification due to their calcium carbonate shells (Zang et al. 2022). Adults are primarily sessile, but can make small-scale movements to avoid predators (Hart and Chute 2004).

Atlantic sea scallops also received a High sensitivity score in the previous assessment (Hare et al. 2016). This score was also driven by Sensitivity to Ocean Acidification and Adult Mobility (Hare et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Mean exposure for Atlantic Sea Scallop across its range was Moderate (2.21 +/- 0.92) (Figure 18.1). Exposure was Very High over Georges Bank and High in southern New England and along the coast of the Gulf of Maine (Figure 18.4). These patterns were driven by bottom temperature, salinity, and oxygen exposures (Figure 18.3).

In the previous Assessment, Atlantic sea scallop received a High exposure score, driven by sea surface temperature and ocean acidification exposure (Hare et al. 2016).

Vulnerability:

Mean Vulnerability for Atlantic Sea Scallop across its range was Moderate (6.62 +/- 2.77) (Figure 18.1). Vulnerability was Very High over Georges Bank and High throughout the coast of the Gulf of Maine and Southern New England (Figure 18.5).

In the previous Assessment, Atlantic Sea Scallop received a High Vulnerability score (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

95% of sensitivity attribute data quality scores were greater than or equal to 2 for both stocks of Atlantic Sea Scallop, indicating excellent data quality. No data gaps (mean attribute data quality score < 2) were found in either stock (Figure 18.1).

Model Confidence Score:

Mean model confidence scores were 2.6 (+/- 0.5) indicating high confidence in the model. Experts stated that important variables matched their expectations. The ensemble model’s AUC was 0.85, indicating good performance.

Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

There were significant declines in sea scallop core habitat area and range between 1993 and 2023. Most core habitat loss occurred in the Mid Atlantic Bight (Figure 18.6). The weighted centroid shifted less than 10 km east-northeast.

Atlantic sea scallop distributions are expected to remain similar in the next decade. No significant changes in habitat area are expected and the weighted centroid of the distribution is predicted to shift less than five kilometers southwest (Figure 18.6).

Sea scallops received a Moderate Distributional Vulnerability Rank in the previous Assessment (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Both stocks of Atlantic sea scallop are estimated to be negatively impacted by future climate change. Certainty ranged from 88 – 99%, with higher certainty for the Mid Atlantic Bight stock. Rising temperatures and ocean acidification induce stress in sea scallops. In addition, anthropogenic activities such as dredging and offshore wind construction degrade habitats and impact behaviors. These results are similar to those from the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

The Atlantic sea scallop population in the Northwest Atlantic is subject to climate-related environmental stress from rising temperatures and ocean acidification (Zang et al. 2022). Scallop habitat can be degraded by trawling/dredging activity (Maguire et al. 2002). Scallop feeding behaviors are temporarily impacted by noise pollution associated with offshore wind construction (J’ez’equel et al. 2022). Changes in temperature and food availability are likely the predominant drivers for reduced growth in sea scallops (Cranford et al. 1998). Rheuban et al. (2018) modeled the Atlantic sea scallop fishery under a range of climate and management scenarios and found the possibility of reduced biomass up to 50% by the end of this century. Results of a scope for growth model with increased warming food deficiency indicated a range contraction of suitable sea scallop habitat in the Mid-Atlantic Bight (Zang et al. 2022). A second scope for growth model by Zang et al. (2023) investigated the relationship between sea scallop size and temperature. Under future climate warming, sea scallops exhibited a range contraction and decline in maximum shell height. An integrated assessment model simulating sea scallop population dynamics with oceanographic and socioeconomic influences indicates that sea scallop harvests could experience a substantial decline by 2050 under increased CO2 emissions (with the assumption that ocean acidification decreases recruitment and slows growth and warming increases growth) (Cooley et al. 2015).

Life History Synopsis

Atlantic sea scallops are one of the most economically important fisheries in the Northwest Atlantic, with average annual ex-vessel revenues of over $500 million (Zang et al. 2022). Their habitat ranges from the Gulf of St. Lawrence to Cape Hatteras, North Carolina (Stewart and Arnold 1994) with primary fishing areas in Georges Bank, the Great South Channel, and the Mid-Atlantic Bight (Hart and Rago 2006). After a period of severe overfishing in the 1970s to 1990s, regulations including effort reductions, gear restrictions, and closed areas were introduced to rebuild the stock (Hart and Rago 2006). Spawning occurs in late summer or early fall, although smaller spawning events at other times of the year are possible (Culliney 1974). Gametes from other scallops, agitation, or tides may trigger scallop spawning (Parsons et al. 1992). Larvae reside in the water column for 5-7 weeks (Thouzeau, Robert, and Smith 1991) and circulate with residual currents ranging from 6-25 cm/s (Culliney 1974). Larval exchange is influenced by duration and depth of planktonic drift, gyre strength, and weak cross-isobath flow (Thouzeau, Robert, and Smith 1991). The transition to the benthos of the freeswimming larvae occurs at a size of about 0.25 mm shell height and is accompanied by drastic changes in diet, morphology, and locomotory ability (Bourne 1965). Mean fall temperatures impact the time larvae take to settle (Tremblay et al. 1994). Sea scallop larvae are potentially preyed on by filter feeders and larval and adult planktivores (Tremblay and Sinclair 1986). Juvenile Atlantic sea scallops are mainly found on gravel, small rocks, shells, and among branching animals and plants that permit attachment of juveniles (Thouzeau, Robert, and Smith 1991) and feed on suspended particles, primarily phytoplankton and microzooplankton (Shumway, Selvin, and Schick 1987). Adult sea scallops are filter feeders on phytoplankton and other suspended organic particulates from the water (Shumway, Selvin, and Schick 1987). Adult sea scallops prefer temperatures of 5-18°C (Hart and Chute 2004). Predation on juvenile and adult sea scallops decreases with shell height; common predators include Atlantic cod, wolffish, sculpins, winter and yellowtail flounder, as well as boring polychaetes, sea stars, crabs, and lobsters (Hart and Chute 2004). The New England Fishery Management Council manages Atlantic sea scallops in federal waters under the Atlantic Sea Scallop Fishery Management Plan. As of the 2020 Management Track Assessment, the stock is not overfished and overfishing is not occurring (Northeast Fisheries Science Center 2020).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

Monthly exposure ranged from Low to High. Scallops experienced Low exposures in late winter and spring (February through May) (Figure 18.9). These patterns were driven by changes to bottom temperature and oxygen exposure. Exposure was High in August, driven by increases in downward carbon flux exposure (Figure 18.8).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Bottom oxygen, salinity, and aragonite saturation, in addition to net primary production were important dynamic drivers of sea scallop distribution (Figure 18.10). Scallops feed on suspended particles (Shumway, Selvin, and Schick 1987) and are often associated with gravel, other shellfish, and rocks (Thouzeau, Robert, and Smith 1991). When only these factors were used to calculate exposure, mean exposure remained Moderate (Figure 18.11; Figure 18.12). High exposure was still found on Georges Bank and in Southern New England (Figure 18.11).
