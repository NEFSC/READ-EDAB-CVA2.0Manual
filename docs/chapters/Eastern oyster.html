Stocks

Eastern Oyster is managed as a single stock or stock information was not considered for this iteration of the CVA.

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

The Eastern oyster received a Very High sensitivity score, with 100% certainty according to bootstrap analysis (Figure 29.1). Five attributes had scores greater than 3.5: Adult Mobility (4), Other Stressors (4), Relationship to Calcified Organisms (3.9), Complexity in Reproductive Strategy (3.8), and Egg and Larval Survival and Settlement Requirements (3.5) (Figure 29.1).

Eastern Oysters are sessile once they settle out of the plankton, and settle in estuarine habitats. As a result, they are exposed to a variety of stressors due to urbanization impacts such as hypoxia, loss of seagrass beds, dredging, and changes to freshwater inputs (Guyant et al. 2022). Spawning is initiated by warmer temperatures. Settling out of the plankton also is dependent on temperature and the presence of conspecifics. Because recruitment is density dependent, population collapse can be sudden.

In the 2016 Assessment, Eastern Oysters received a High sensitivity score. This was driven by Sensitivity to Ocean Acidification, Adult Mobility, and Other Stressors, similar to our results (Hare et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Mean exposure for Eastern Oyster was Low (1.33 +/- 0.66) (Figure 29.1). Scores were Moderate within Chesapeake Bay but were Low on the continental shelf (Figure 29.4). This was driven by high exposure to bottom temperature and oxygen changes within the Bay (Figure 29.3).

In the previous assessment, eastern oysters received a Very High exposure score, driven by sea surface temperature, air temperature, and ocean acidification (Hare et al. 2016).

Vulnerability:

Mean vulnerability for Eastern Oyster was Moderate (5.25 +/- 2.63) (Figure 29.1). Vulnerability was highest in the Chesapeake Bay (Figure 29.5).

In the previous Assessment, Eastern Oyster received a Very High Vulnerability score, driven by high and very high sensitivity and exposure scores, respectively (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

90% of data quality scores were greater than or equal to 2, indicating excellent data quality. Population Growth Rate and Stock Size/Status were identified as data gaps (mean attribute data quality score < 2) (Figure 29.1).

Model Confidence Score:

The eastern oyster ensemble model received a 1.2 (+/- 0.9) mean model confidence score, indicating low confidence in the model. Poor sample size drove down model confidence scores and the model struggled to predict nearshore habitats. The ensemble AUC score for the eastern oyster model was 0.95, indicating that the model had excellent ability to predict oyster presence/absence. ## Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

Eastern oyster habitat was increasing between 1993 and 2015, but recently declined significantly (Figure 29.6). The weighted centroid shifted 28 km west-southwest.

Under near future climate conditions, these trends are predicted to continue. Further habitat loss is predicted across the species range and the weighted centroid is predicted to shift a further 21 km southwest (Figure 29.6).

In the previous Assessment, the eastern oyster received a Moderate Distributional Vulnerability Rank score (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Experts predicted that climate change would have a neutral effect on Eastern Oysters. Certainty was low at 60%. While oysters live in vulnerable estuarine habitats (Guyant et al. 2022), spawning occurs throughout the year when temperatures are warm (Berringan et al. 1991). Aquaculture programs also help contribute to population recovery (MacKenzie 1981). In the previous Assessment, experts predicted that climate change would have a negative impact on Eastern Oysters (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

Climate change, warmer temperatures, and perhaps different tidal ranges, may increase competition for resources with current competitors, like hooked mussel, invasive species such as Didemnum sp., or increase exposure to predators like veined rapa whelk (White and Wilson 1996). Depletion of oyster beds will also likely have negative effects for many marine species and habitats, for they provide refuge and homes for many fish and invertebrates species and help prevent erosion (Eastern Oyster Biological Review Team (EOBRT) 2007). Hurricanes can cover oysters in silt, wash them off beds, or impose extended “freshettes” on oyster beds to the degree that they are significantly impaired and need restoration. This may become more problematic, for hurricanes are thought to increase with increased warming temperatures and climate change (Perret et al. 1999). Since adult oysters are sessile and inhabit mainly highly vulnerable habitats in areas with high climate exposure, their spawning habitat is also highly vulnerable (Farr et al. 2021).

Life History Synopsis

Eastern oysters are found along the eastern U.S. from the Gulf of St. Lawrence to the Gulf of America. They inhabit brackish and salty waters, forming beds or reefs by attaching to each other or other hard substrate (Ozbay et al. 2014). Eastern oysters are a keystone species, and provide valuable habitat for benthic and intertidal organisms. Oysters are sessile filter feeders, contributing to increased water quality through reduced sediment suspension and nutrient uptake and sequestration (Ozbay et al. 2014). Eastern oyster populations in the Mid-Atlantic and Gulf declined in the 19th and 20th centuries, mainly from anthropogenic factors such as land use, habitat degradation, and overharvesting. Disease mortality from Dermo and MSX continues to diminish oyster recovery (Eastern Oyster Biological Review Team (EOBRT) 2007). Aquaculture production of oysters contributes to population recovery and habitat restoration programs across the East Coast (MacKenzie 1981). Oysters spawn during the summer in the North and Mid-Atlantic and throughout most of the year in the South Atlantic when temperatures remain above 20°C and salinities above 10ppt (Berringan et al. 1991). The first larval stage (trochophore) is formed four to six hours following fertilization. Larvae normally settle two to three weeks after hatching, but can last up to 2 months (Eastern Oyster Biological Review Team (EOBRT) 2007). Larvae move both by passive and active (as development progresses) transport, although larval transport in estuaries seems to be minimal. A balance is reached where the larvae can find suitable areas to settle while still being retained in estuaries (Eastern Oyster Biological Review Team (EOBRT) 2007). Settlement ends at the end of the larval stage, and the larval duration is highly temperature dependent (Shumway 1996). Fritz et al. (1984) found that planktotrophic oyster larvae fed upon a variety of phytoplankton taxa (coccoids, centrate diatoms, pennate diatoms, dinoflagellates, and flagellates). Baldwin and Newell (1991) found that the larvae are actually omnivorous, preying on both autotrophic and heterotrophic plankton. Predation on oyster larvae is extremely high by filter-feeders in the water column and in the benthos. Along the Atlantic coast, drills have been responsible for mortality on 33% of spat in Long Island Sound. Oysters can compete with other mussels and barnacles for shell and substrate spaces, resulting in increased mortality when competitors are abundant (Kennedy 1996). Juveniles and adults feed primarily on phytoplankton and suspended detritus, relying on food-laden water being moved past them in order to extract food from the water column (Langdon and Newell 1996). Maturity occurs between 1 and 3 years of age (Florida State University Marine Lab n.d.). Several ages have been proposed, the most common being 20 years. Oysters in Maryland appear to survive at least 10 years based on plantings at restoration sites which are not thought to have received natural spat sets. It is likely that the maximum longevity is substantially longer than 10 years (Maryland Department of Natural Resources 2025). Wild and aquaculture harvest is managed by individual jurisdictions or states, and management guidelines vary (Ozbay et al. 2014).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

Exposure for the eastern oyster varied throughout the year (Figure 29.9). Exposure was Low from March through August due to low bottom temperature and oxygen exposure (Figure 29.8). For the remainder of the year, exposure was Moderate to High (Figure 29.9).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Bottom salinity and aragonite solubility were important drivers of species distribution (Figure 29.10). Eastern Oysters live in estuarine habitats and build calcium carbonate shells (Ozbay et al. 2014). When these factors were used to calculate exposure, scores were Low (1.03 +/- 0.22) (Figure 29.12, Figure 29.11).
