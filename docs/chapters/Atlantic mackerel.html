Stocks

Atlantic mackerel is managed as a single stock or stock information was not considered for this iteration of the CVA.

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

Atlantic mackerel received a Low sensitivity score with a certainty of 100% (Figure 16.1). The only score greater than 2.5 was Stock Size (3.2) (Figure 16.1). Stocks are not currently overfished or experiencing overfishing according to the most recent stock assessment. The stock is rebuilding to target levels (Northeast Fisheries Science Center 2025).

In the previous Climate Vulnerability Assessment, mackerel were given a Moderate sensitivity score (Hare et al. 2016). This previous score was driven by Stock Size, Egg and Larval Survival and Settlement Requirements (Early Life History Requirements), and Spawning Cycle. Stock sizes were not classified at the time of the last assessment, and it was unclear how spawning cycle and early life stages would be impacted by warming waters (Hare et al. 2016). Recent work has suggested that spawning habitats will likely shift west and north under future warming conditions (Bruge et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Atlantic mackerel had an average Exposure score of 3 (+/- 0.25), resulting in a High Exposure score (Figure 16.1). Scores were consistent across the shelf, with the highest exposure observed on Georges Bank (Figure 16.4). These scores were driven by sea surface temperature, salinity, and pH exposure scores (Figure 16.3).

In the previous Assessment, mackerel also received a High exposure score, driven by high exposure to changes in sea surface temperatures and pH (Hare et al. 2016).

Vulnerability:

The mean overall Vulnerability score was Low (3 +/- 0.25) (Figure 16.1). Scores were low throughout the continental shelf with the exception of Georges Bank, where scores were moderate on portions of the Bank (Figure 16.5).

In the previous Assessment, mackerel received a Moderate vulnerability score, driven in part by a moderate sensitivity score (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

95% of sensitivity attribute data quality scores were greater than or equal to 2, indicating excellent data quality. Only Relationship to Calcified Organisms was considered a data gap (mean attribute data quality score < 2).

Model Confidence Score:

The Atlantic mackerel ensemble species distribution model received a mean model confidence score of 1.4 (+/- 0.5), indicating low confidence in the model. Experts noted that the model missed known seasonal shifts in distribution, but also highlighted that mackerel distributions are patchy and diffuse. The ensemble model’s AUC was 0.73, indicating fair performance.

Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

Mackerel habitats have been highly variable between 1993 and 2023. Habitat areas declined around 2010 and have since recovered to near 1993 levels. The southern edge of mackerel ranges have moved northward (Figure 16.6). The weighted centroid shifted 16 km northeast between 1993 and 2023.

Mackerel habitats are predicted to expand in future ocean conditions. Both core habitat area and mackerel range are predicted to increase. While the southern edge of the range is predicted to continue to move northward, mackerel are expected to move into new areas in southern New England (Figure 16.6). Distributions are predicted to continue to shift eastward by 5 km.

In the previous Assessment, mackerel received a High Distributional Vulnerability rank, due to their high mobility, migrations, and high larval dispersal (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Atlantic mackerel are likely to be negatively impacted by climate change. Expert certainty was 83%. Regional warming has resulted in northeasterly shifts in mackerel distributions. Spawning habitat could increase in more northern regions such as the Gulf of St Lawrence as mackerel seek out preferred temperatures. This result is similar to the results from the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment for the region (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

Atlantic mackerel are sensitive to temperature changes, particularly temperatures greater than 5°C (Overholtz, Hare, and Keith 2011). From 1968 to 2008, the distribution of mackerel in the Northwest Atlantic has shifted roughly 250km north and east in addition to changes in depth distribution to more shallow on-shelf waters. These changes in position and depth are known to be correlated with the gradual warming of the Northwest Atlantic (Overholtz, Hare, and Keith 2011). Atlantic mackerel egg distribution has also shifted northeastward as a consequence of warming, depleting egg production in important spawning locations in southern New England (Richardson et al. 2020). Bruge et al. (2016) projected changes in Northeast Atlantic mackerel spawning distribution under future climate scenarios, and found that the spawning center of gravity is expected to shift westward and northward. Mbaye et al. (2019) investigated effects of environmental conditions on Atlantic mackerel spawning habitat in the Gulf of St. Lawrence. Generalized additive model (GAM) results showed that spawning habitat in the Gulf of St. Lawrence could expand and extend to western and southern Newfoundland.

Life History Synopsis

Atlantic mackerel in the Northwest Atlantic inhabit cold and temperate continental shelf waters from the Gulf of St Lawrence to Cape Lookout, North Carolina (Studholme 1999). Spawning occurs from April to August in the Northwest Atlantic and progresses from south to north with warming temperatures and following migration patterns (Sette 1943). There are two spawning contingents: the southern contingent spawns from April to July in the Mid-Atlantic Bight and Gulf of Maine, and the northern contingent spawns in the southern Gulf of St. Lawrence in June and July (Berrien 1982) (Morse, Fahay, and Smith 1987). Temperature is a cue for spawning. Spawning does not occur until water has warmed to 8°C with most egg production occurring between 9-14°C (Sette 1943). More recent work has shown that mackerel spawning and migration timing is negatively correlated with sea surface temperature (Jansen and Gislason 2011). Early life stage development happens quickly, with eggs hatching in 4-7.5 hours, depending on temperature (Berrien 1982). Larvae (< 13 mm) occur primarily in offshore waters from Chesapeake Bay to southern Gulf of St. Lawrence (Sette 1943). Larval and juvenile depth varies diurnally and with age (Collette and Klein-MacPhee 2002). Larvae feed primarily on zooplankton and copepods, and larvae > 6.4 mm are known to experience cannibalistic behavior (Castonguay et al. 2008). Juveniles rely on water column habitats, both on the shelf, and in estuaries (Guyant et al. 2022). Juveniles and adults are opportunistic feeders that prey on a wide range of crustaceans, copepods, planktonic organisms, and fishes (Sette 1950). Squid, seabirds and larger piscivores, including Atlantic cod, prey on juvenile mackerel (Studholme 1999). Adults over winter in moderately deep water, 70-200 m, along the continental shelf. After migrating inshore in April-May, they form the two spawning aggregations (Berrien 1982). Predators of adult mackerel include larger fish, sharks, skates, seabirds, and marine mammals (primarily whales and seals) (Studholme 1999). Atlantic mackerel are managed by the Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council under the Mackerel, Squid, and Butterfish Fishery Management Plan. As of the 2025 assessment, the stock is not overfished and overfishing is not occurring, but is rebuilding to target levels (Northeast Fisheries Science Center 2025).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

Monthly exposure was consistent for Atlantic mackerel. Exposure was High most of the year, with the exception of April and May, where exposure declined to Moderate due to declines in sea surface temperature exposure (Figure 16.9, Figure 16.8).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Sea surface temperatures and salinities were the major drivers of mackerel distributions (Figure 16.10). Mackerel are sensitive to sea surface temperatures (Overholtz, Hare, and Keith 2011; Jansen and Gislason 2011), and juveniles rely on estuarine habitats where they will be exposed to changes in salinity (Guyant et al. 2022). When exposure was calculated with only these factors, the mean exposure score decreased to Moderate (1.71 +/- 0.85) (Figure 16.12). Exposure scores were greatest in the Gulf of Maine and on the mid continental shelf where high exposure to temperature and salinity changes overlapped (Figure 16.11).
