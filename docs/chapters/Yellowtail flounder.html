Biological Sensitivity:

The Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic and Georges Bank stocks of Yellowtail flounder both received Moderate sensitivity scores. Both scores had 88% certainty. These scores were driven by Stock Size (4 and 3.6), Spawning Cycle (2.7 and 2.6) and Egg and Larval Survival and Settlement Requirements (2.6). Adult Mobility (2.5) also played a role in the score for the Georges Bank stock.

The Gulf of Maine/Cape Cod stock of Yellowtail flounder received a Low sensitivity score, with 56% certainty. For this stock, only the Stock Size (2.8) attribute had a score greater than 2.5.

Spawning occurs from mid-February to July on Georges Bank and in nearshore habitats in Southern New England and the Mid-Atlantic (Martin and Drewry 1978). Larvae feed on zooplankton prey and zooplankton abundance has been linked to recruitment success. Although it is also worth noting that studies directly testing for mismatches did not find evidence of mismatches between larvae and zooplankton overlap and recruitment (J. Kittel 2025; Johnson et al. 1999). Larvae perform vertical migrations to help increase retention in and around nursery locations. Recruitment has also been correlated with circulation patterns and the Mid Atlantic Cold Pool. Both the Georges Bank and Southern New England stocks are under rebuilding plans (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2025c).

In the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment, yellowtail flounder also received a Low sensitivity score. Similar to the Gulf of Maine/Cape Cod stock, the only attribute that scored above 2.5 in the 2016 Assessment was Stock Status (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Experts predicted that the effects of climate change on Yellowtail flounder would be negative for both the Georges Bank and Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic stocks. Certainty for both stocks was high at 98% and 100% respectively. In the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment, experts also predicted that yellowtail would be negatively impacted by climate change (Hare et al. 2016). For the Gulf of Maine/Cape Cod stock, the effect was predicted to be neutral, but with lower certainty (86%). Increased temperatures have been linked to decreased productivity, increased larval mortality, and reduced settlement rates (J. A. Kittel et al. 2026; Klein, Smith, and Kritzer 2017). Recruitment has also been linked to the Mid-Atlantic Cold Pool (J. Kittel 2025).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

Yellowtail flounder abundance, distribution, and recruitment have been associated with a number of environmental and climate indices. Rapid warming of the Northwest Atlantic has resulted in decreased productivity in yellow flounder (J. A. Kittel et al. 2026). Higher temperatures can increase mortality in yellowtail flounder larvae, and faster rates of warming causing earlier stratification may reduce settlement rates in juveniles (Klein, Smith, and Kritzer 2017). Yellowtail recruitment in Southern New England is highly correlated with the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) and air temperature (Sullivan, Cowen, and Steves 2005). Low recruitment since 1990 was associated with unfavorable environmental conditions caused by a northward shift in the Gulf Stream (Xu 2016; J. Kittel 2025). The Mid-Atlantic Cold Pool, bottom temperature, and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO) are also linked to recruitment (J. Kittel 2025). As warming continues, joint management between the United States and Canada may be more challenging as distributions change (Sumaila, Palacios-Abrantes, and Cheung 2020).

Life History Synopsis

Yellowtail flounder are found in the Northwest Atlantic from Newfoundland to Chesapeake Bay, inhabiting sandy bottom waters between 130-230 m depth (Johnson et al. 1999) and at temperatures of -1 to 18°C (Overholtz and Cadrin 1998). Previous research has suggested that fish live up to 17 years, possibly as high as 20 years, although fish older than 7 years is unusual (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2012). The fishery is divided into 3 stocks: Gulf of Maine/Cape Cod, Georges Bank, and Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2025a, 2025b, 2025c). Most females are mature by ages 2-3 (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2012). Spawning occurs from mid-February to July along the continental shelf waters of Georges Bank, northwest of Cape Cod, Southern New England and nearshore along New Jersey and southern Long Island (Martin and Drewry 1978). Spawning peaks from April to June in southern New England and late June to July in Canada (Berrien and Sibunka 1999). Laboratory studies show eggs begin to hatch 6-7 days after fertilization (Benoit and Pepin 1999). At sea hatching times range from 14.5 days at 4⁰C to 4.5 days at 14⁰C (Walsh and Morgan 2004). Eggs are pelagic (most at 10-90m depth), with movement limited to currents in spawning locations (Smith, Sibunka, and Wells 1975). Larvae move from the surface down to about 20 m, with larger larvae making longer migrations. This habit may assist in the dispersal of larvae because circulation at the surface transports the larvae further and is likely associated with feeding behavior (Smith, Sibunka, and Wells 1978). Larvae feed on zooplankton, particularly Calanus (J. Kittel 2025). Once larvae reach metamorphosis size (11.6-16 mm) bottom temperature (4-8°C) and depth (41-70m) are the most important parameters for determining settlement (Collette and Klein-MacPhee 2002). Juveniles utilize marine and estuarine sandy and muddy substrates, as well as estuarine and marine water columns (Collette and Klein-MacPhee 2002). Rotifers, copepods, and brine shrimp nauplii were fed to juveniles in laboratory studies (Benoit and Pepin 1999). Calanus copepods are considered a critical food source for larvae/juveniles (Purchase, Boyce, and Brown 2000). Amphipods (41% by weight) and polychaetes (38.5%) dominate the diet of adult yellowtail, but they also can consume cnidarians, small crabs and shrimps, mysids, cumaceans, isopods, univalve and bivalve mollusks, echinoderms, and sometimes small fish (Bowman et al. 1987, 2000). Similar to other groundfish, yellowtail may be impacted by increasing grey seal and dogfish populations and resulting increased predation pressures (J. Kittel 2025). Other predators of juvenile and adult yellowtail include spiny dogfish, skates, cod, hakes, and monkfish (Johnson et al. 1999). Minimal migration patterns show that Southern New England yellowtail traveled eastward during spring and summer and westward in the fall and winter as a result of seasonal changes in temperature (Smith, Sibunka, and Wells 1975). Recent tagging studies, however, show that individuals generally don’t move between stocks (Goethel, Legault, and Cadrin 2015). Yellowtail flounder are managed by the New England Fishery Management Council through the Northeast Multispecies (Groundfish) Fishery Management Plan. The Georges Bank stock is jointly managed by the Council and Canada, as the stock extends beyond the international boundary. The Cape Cod/Gulf of Maine stock is not overfished and not subject to overfishing (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2025a). The Georges Bank stock is overfished but under a rebuilding plan; overfishing status is unknown (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2025b). The Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic stock is overfished but under a rebuilding plan, but not subject to overfishing (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2025c).
