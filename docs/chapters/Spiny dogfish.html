Stocks

Spiny dogfish is managed as a single stock or stock information was not considered for this iteration of the CVA.

Score Descriptions:

Sensitivity:

Spiny dogfish received a Low sensitivity score, with a certainty of 100%. The only attribute with a score greater than 2.5 was Population Growth Rate (3.6) (Figure 43.1). They are a long-lived, slowly maturing species. Age at maturity ranges from 6 years for males and 12 years for females, with maximum lifespans of 35-40 years (Stehlik 2007). Gestation periods last from 18-24 months (Stehlik 2007).

Spiny dogfish also received a Low sensitivity score in the previous Climate Vulnerability Assessment. This score was also driven by Population Growth Rates (Hare et al. 2016).

Exposure:

Mean Exposure scores for Spiny dogfish were Moderate (2 +/- 0.94) (Figure 43.1). Exposure was Very High over Georges Bank and High along the coast of the Gulf of Maine and in Southern New England. Scores were also High on the continental shelf break (Figure 43.4). These patterns were driven by bottom temperature, oxygen, salinity, and aragonite saturation scores (Figure 43.3).

In the previous Assessment, spiny dogfish received a High Exposure score, driven by high sea surface temperature and ocean acidification exposure scores (Hare et al. 2016).

Vulnerability:

Mean Vulnerability scores for Spiny dogfish were Low (2 +/- 0.94) (Figure 43.1). Exposure was mostly uniform across the model species range. There were some Moderate scores over Georges Bank (Figure 43.5).

In the previous Assessment, spiny dogfish also received a Low Vulnerability score (Hare et al. 2016).

Data Quality Metrics

Life History Data Quality:

90% of data quality scores were greater than or equal to 2, indicating excellent data quality. No data gaps (mean attribute data quality score < 2) were identified (Figure 43.1).

Model Confidence Score:

The ensemble spiny dogfish model received a mean model confidence score of 0.9 (+/- 0.3), indicating no confidence in the model. The predicted distributions did not meet experts’ expectations. Experts suggested that the model may be overfit. The ensemble model AUC score was 0.77, indicating fair model performance.

Additional Metrics

Distribution Shifts:

Spiny dogfish core habitat and range shrunk significantly between 1993 and 2023. Spiny dogfish lost core habitat off the coast of Virginia and the southern edge of their range moved northward during this time period (Figure 43.6). The weighted centroid shifted 10 km north-northeast.

Under near future ocean conditions, spiny dogfish core habitat and range are predicted to expand to near 1993 distributions, including reestablishing core habitat off the coast of Virginia (Figure 43.6). The weighted centroid is predicted to shift 10 km back southwest.

In the previous assessment, spiny dogfish received a Very High Distributional Vulnerability Rank due to their habitat preferences and high mobility (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Experts predict that climate change will have a neutral effect on spiny dogfish. Certainty was 91%. Occurrence of spiny dogfish is correlated with warmer temperatures (Sagarese, Frisk, Cerrato, et al. 2014). They are highly migratory and will move north-south to follow waters within their preferred temperature range (6-13C) (Stehlik 2007) (Carlson et al. 2014). They may benefit from the loss of cod as they occupy similar feeding niches (Neuenhoff et al. 2019) (Ono et al. 2019). The previous Assessment also predicted that climate change would have a neutral impact on spiny dogfish (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

Nye et al. (2009) observed a range expansion and southward shift in center of biomass in spiny dogfish, although changes in biomass over the study period may have influenced these shifts. Seasonal occurrence of spiny dogfish is highly correlated with depth and bottom temperature, with a higher probability of occurrence in warmer years versus colder years (Sagarese, Frisk, Cerrato, et al. 2014). Spiny dogfish shift their distribution seasonally; variation in environmental drivers may impact population and fishery dynamics as temperatures continue to increase. Climate-inducing seasonal distribution shifts influencing habitat availability for spiny dogfish may also lead to vulnerability of the inshore fishery in the fall (Sagarese, Frisk, Miller, et al. 2014).

Life History Synopsis

Atlantic spiny dogfish are a schooling, highly migratory shark species (HMS) inhabiting inshore and offshore waters from Labrador to Florida, although they are most abundant from Nova Scotia and Cape Hatteras. Spiny dogfish perform diel vertical migrations; most schools are found around 40-50 m depth, but can descend upwards of 500 m (Stehlik 2007) (Carlson et al. 2014). There are populations that occupy the continental shelves of Europe, Asia, and the northeast Pacific, but these populations do not mix (Stehlik 2007). Spiny dogfish perform north-south migrations and move between inshore-offshore waters in response to water temperature, migrating north in the spring and summer and south in the fall and winter to remain in waters between 6-13°C (Stehlik 2007) (Carlson et al. 2014). Age at maturity is 6 years for males and 12 years for females, with a lifespan of 35 years for males and 40 years for females. Spawning occurs offshore in the winter, with a gestation period of 18-24 months (Stehlik 2007). Proportions of mature females in catch are correlated with bottom temperatures, with more mature females caught at relatively higher bottom temperatures. However, how temperature impacts reproduction is still unclear (Sagarese, Frisk, Miller, et al. 2014). Spiny dogfish are opportunistic feeders, preying upon a variety of fishes (herring, mackerel, hakes, sand lance, menhaden), squid, ctenophores, polychaetes, crustaceans, bivalves. Diets can shift based on location and availability (Stehlik 2007) (DeVries, Gartland, and Latour 2025). Predators of spiny dogfish include sharks, seals, cod, goosefish, dolphins, and larger fish (Stehlik 2007). Spiny dogfish occupy a similar niche to that of Atlantic cod, whose populations have been impacted by increasing seal populations in the Northwest Atlantic and Canada. It has been hypothesized that the loss of cod could be a benefit to spiny dogfish, since they occupy similar feeding niches (Neuenhoff et al. 2019) (Ono et al. 2019). The Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council (MAFMC) leads the joint management of the Spiny Dogfish Fishery Management Plan, with support from the New England Fishery Management Council (NEFMC) and Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission (ASMFC) (Mid-Atlantic Fishery Management Council (MAFMC) 2025). The ASMFC manages spiny dogfish in state waters via the Interstate Fishery Management Plan for Spiny Dogfish (Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission (ASMFC) 2002). As of 2023, spiny dogfish in the Northwest Atlantic are not overfished and overfishing is not occurring (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2023).

Supplemental Scores

Exposure Timeseries

There was little monthly variation in Exposure scores for spiny dogfish (Figure 43.9). Scores ranged from Low to Moderate and were lowest from February through May due to low bottom temperature and oxygen exposure scores (Figure 43.8).

Exposure due to Significant Factors

Bottom temperature, oxygen, and salinity, as well as large zooplankton biomass were important dynamic drivers of spiny dogfish distributions (Figure 43.10). Temperatures drive migration patterns (Stehlik 2007) (Carlson et al. 2014). Spiny dogfish are also generalist piscivores (Stehlik 2007) (DeVries, Gartland, and Latour 2025). When exposure was calculated using only these important factors, scores remained Moderate and distributions of scores remained relatively similar (Figure 43.12; Figure 43.11).
