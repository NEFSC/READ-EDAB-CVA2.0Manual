Biological Sensitivity:

The Georges Bank and Gulf of Maine stocks of Winter Flounder both received Moderate sensitivity scores. Certainty for both stocks was 58%. For these stocks, five attributes had scores greater than 2.5: Complexity in Reproductive Strategy (3.3 and 3.4), Egg and Larval Survival and Settlement Requirements (2.7 and 2.9), Other Stressors (2.6 and 2.9), Spawning Cycle (2.8), and Stock Size (3 and 2.7).

The Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic stock received a High sensitivity score, with a certainty of 91%. Three attributes had scores greater than 3: Complexity in Reproductive Strategy (3.5), Egg and Larval Survival and Settlement Requirements (3.2), and Other Stressors (3.1).

Winter flounder spawn in winter and early spring before waters reach temperatures of 6C (Pereira 1999). Spawning occurs in nearshore and estuarine habitats, including muddy and sandy substrates, and native salt marsh habitats which are considered highly vulnerable (Guyant et al. 2022). Larvae are dependent on bottom currents to help retain them in and around spawning areas and nursery habitats. Adults depend on estuarine habitats, which may be impacted by hypoxia and changes to freshwater inputs (Guyant et al. 2022). Predators include striped bass, spiny dogfish, and seals, which have all experienced distribution shifts that could result in increased predation pressure (Pereira 1999).

These results are similar to those from the 2016 Climate Vulnerability Assessment where winter flounder were given a High sensitivity score. This score was also driven by Stock Status and Early Life History Requirements (Hare et al. 2016).

Directional Effect:

Climate change was predicted to negatively impact all three stocks of winter flounder. Certainties ranged from 58% - 100% across stocks, with the lowest certainty for the Gulf of Maine stock and the highest for the Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic stock. Increasing temperatures have been linked to poor spring recruitment and higher summer time mortality, in addition to shifts in migration timing (Able et al. 2014; Langan 2021; Klein, Smith, and Kritzer 2017). The Southern New England/Mid Atlantic stock has also shifted norward (Able et al. 2014; Langan 2021). The previous Assessment also predicted that winter flounder would be negatively impacted by climate change (Hare et al. 2016).

Climate Effects on Abundance and Distribution

A number of studies describe evidence of changes in winter flounder growth, abundance, and distribution as a result of climate change. The Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic Bight stock declined sharply in the 1980s from overfishing, has not recovered, and is shifting northward (Able et al. 2014; Langan 2021). Warm spring temperatures have resulted in poor recruitment (Able et al. 2014), and declining productivity due to warming estuarine waters has blocked recovery efforts (Bell et al. 2014). Elevated water temperatures can cause faster development rates of early life history winter flounder; length of incubation time is crucial as a nearshore and estuarine species as these areas are more susceptible to anthropogenic impacts (Klein, Smith, and Kritzer 2017). Increasing temperatures may alter predation rates, affecting survival of larval winter flounder, as well as migration and distribution patterns (Klein, Smith, and Kritzer 2017). In Narragansett Bay, warmer summer temperatures were linked to increasing mortality in young-of-the-year winter flounder. Greater abundance of mature winter flounder in the lower bay during spring may also indicate a potential shift in spawning migration timing (Langan 2021).

Life History Synopsis

Winter flounder are found in estuaries and on the continental shelf from Nova Scotia to North Carolina, and are most common in the Mid-Atlantic north of Delaware Bay (Pereira 1999). They prefer temperatures between 0-20°C and depths less than 30m inshore and 100m offshore (Pereira 1999). The fishery is divided into 3 stocks: Gulf of Maine, Georges Bank, and Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic. They reach maturity at 2- 3 years, varying across stocks with southern populations maturing younger than northern ones, and live to a maximum age of 15 years (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2011). Winter flounder spawn inshore in the winter through spring, peaking in February/March in the Mid-Atlantic and Southern New England and continuing into May in the Gulf of Maine (Pereira 1999). Timing of spawning aggregations is linked to inshore migration timing, which is complex and possibly related to time of year, temperature, lunar cycle, and photoperiod, as well as varying across years. Individuals migrate inshore in the fall, and migrate offshore in the spring. The Georges Bank population does not migrate (Pearcy 1962). Females deposit eggs at night and on bottom substrates, usually sand or algal mats, and can deposit multiple batches of eggs per spawning season (Ziegler, Zacharias, and Frisk 2019). Eggs are demersal and hatch within 2-3 weeks, depending on temperature. Larvae are initially planktonic but become more demersal as they metamorphosize, which starts 5-6 weeks after hatching. Larvae rely on bottom currents to keep them in and around spawning areas in inshore bays and estuaries. They are tolerant to a wide range of temperatures, salinities, and dissolved oxygen concentrations (Pearcy 1962). Metamorphosis is complete about 8 weeks after hatching, and is temperature dependent (Pereira 1999). Size, rather than age, tends to drive metamorphosis and settlement (Manderson et al. 2003). Larvae consume a variety of planktonic prey including nauplii, invertebrate eggs, calanoids, and polychaetes, with larger larvae consuming larger prey. Copepods and other zooplankton are important during metamorphosis (Stehlik and Meise 2000). Small medusae are important predators to larvae. Larvae can also be preyed upon by amphipods and Atlantic mackerel (Pereira 1999). Juveniles utilize marine and estuarine rocky, sand, and mud substrates, particularly fine sand and gravel as well as marine and estuarine water columns. They also occupy submerged aquatic vegetation, marshes, oyster reefs, and macroalgae habitats (Bigelow and Schroeder 1953). Juveniles consume a variety of prey including copepods, amphipods, and polychaetes. The diversity of prey increases and the importance of smaller zooplankton decreases with size (Pearcy 1962; Pereira 1999). Adults are omnivorous opportunistic feeders. They feed during the day as they depend on sight to locate prey. Their diet is limited by mouth size; diets primarily consist of polychaetes and crustaceans (mostly amphipods), but also include bivalves, fish eggs, and fish. Diets can shift based on available prey (Stehlik and Meise 2000; Pereira 1999). Predators of adult winter flounder include striped bass, spiny dogfish, and seals (Pereira 1999). The New England Fishery Management Council manages winter flounder in federal waters under the Northeast Multispecies (Groundfish) Fishery Management Plan. The Atlantic States Marine Fisheries Commission coordinates management in state waters. None of the 3 stocks are subject to overfishing. The Georges Bank and Southern New England/Mid-Atlantic stocks are not overfished; the Gulf of Maine stock has not been assessed and population abundance is unknown (Northeast Fisheries Science Center (NEFSC) 2025b, 2025c, 2025a).
